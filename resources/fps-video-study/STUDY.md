# How the "Fable 5.2" Three.js FPS gets its look

Study of the video Kesh shared on 2026-11-11
(x.com/Stargridcompass/status/2120356340778648060). Report only: nothing in the game changed.

**What the video is.** 3 min 46 s, 1920x1080 at 60 fps. It is a repost: the poster writes (in
Chinese) that the game is built on Three.js and that "the 3D scene, character animation and
shooting effects were all generated by Fable 5.2", then asks whether the video itself might be
AI-made. A browser full-screen notice shows in the first frame and on the pause screen, so it
does run in a browser. I can't check who made it, what prompts or hand work went into it, what
GPU recorded it, or its frame rate (no fps counter shows). It is a 5v5 team deathmatch
("first to 40") in one hotel map: atrium lobby, bar, kitchen, corridors and a rooftop terrace
at sunset.

Frames are under `frames/` (four contact sheets, one frame every 5 s, plus eight full frames).

## The main point

The models are not where the quality comes from. Look past the light and most of the hotel is
plain shapes: the sofas are rounded boxes, the pillars are cylinders, the city outside is boxes
with a window texture, and the plants are flat cards. The guns and arms are the most detailed
models, and they are the only thing that stays on screen all the time.

What makes it read as "AAA" is lighting, materials and post-processing all working toward one
look: a single low golden-hour sun, glossy floors, warm grading, and a camera at eye height
that keeps those glossy floors and long shadows in view. That matches the answer in chat,
with one correction. Baked lightmaps and authored GLTF are possible, but nothing on screen
needs them. Everything I can see could be built in code with stock Three.js parts, which fits
the claim that a model wrote it.

## What each costs on Kesh's office laptop (i7-1255U, Iris Xe)

These are **estimates**, not measurements. They come from typical Three.js costs on Iris Xe-class
integrated GPUs at 1080p, and the Iris Xe shares memory bandwidth with the CPU, so passes
that read full-screen buffers cost more there than on a desktop card. Our frame budget at
60 fps is 16.7 ms, and the office laptop already drops frames today.

| Technique | Estimated cost on Iris Xe, 1080p | Note |
|---|---|---|
| One sun shadow map (2048, PCF soft) | 1.5 to 3 ms | Re-draws every shadow caster once more |
| Planar floor reflection | 4 to 8 ms (a second full scene draw) | Our biggest risk; scales with draw calls |
| Screen-space reflections | 3 to 8 ms | Noisy at low sample counts |
| SSAO / GTAO at half resolution | 2 to 5 ms | |
| Depth of field | 1 to 3 ms | |
| Motion blur | 1 to 2 ms | Needs a velocity buffer to look right |
| Chromatic aberration, vignette, grade | under 0.3 ms | One full-screen pass, which we already run for the grade |
| Bloom | 1 to 2 ms | We already pay this |
| PBR instead of toon shading | 0.5 to 2 ms more per lit pixel | Grows with the number of lights |
| Environment map (PMREM) | about 0 per frame; built once at load | |
| Emissive fake lights | about 0 | |
| Decals and sprite particles | under 1 ms if pooled | We already pool VFX (`fxpool.js`) |
| Baked lightmaps or vertex AO | 0 per frame | It costs load time or authoring time instead |

The video's full stack (shadows, reflections, AO, DoF, motion blur, bloom, PBR) adds up to
somewhere around 15 to 25 ms on Iris Xe at 1080p. That is under 30 fps before any game logic
runs. It almost certainly ran on a desktop GPU, and on a small fixed map with about 10 actors.

## What we have today

From `src/render/stage.js` and the scenes: a warm key light plus a cool hemisphere fill; toon
materials with a 3-step ramp, ink outlines and facet tones (`docs/MODEL_STANDARD.md`); **no
shadow maps** (filled contact blobs under every body, `src/scenes/arena.js`); no environment
map; post is RenderPass, UnrealBloom (0.9), OutputPass (ACES), a grade pass (vignette, split
tone, warm lift) and FXAA or MSAA. The camera looks down on the floor from above, with up to
seven heroes, packs of foes and dense VFX on screen. Readability is the rule
(`.claude/rules/visuals.md`, `docs/VFX_HIT_RULES.md`).

## What could fit our top-down procedural style

| Option | Fits? | Risk | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. A real sun shadow map**, fitted to the fixed top-down view, with heroes, foes and tall props casting | Good | Medium | It is the single biggest "grounded" cue in the video. Our camera is fixed, so one tight shadow frustum covers the room, which is cheaper than an FPS needs. Risks: shadows banding against the toon ramp, floor telegraphs sitting in shade (readability), 1.5 to 3 ms on the office laptop, so it would need a graphics-setting toggle. It is render-only, so the goldens are untouched. |
| **B. Baked AO in the procedural dressings** (darken vertex colours where props meet the floor and walls, computed once when a room is built) | Good | Low | It gives the "objects sit in the room" look of #8 for no per-frame cost, and it is in the same family as the facet tone step we already put in vertex colours. |
| **C. One golden-hour grade per land** (a stronger, deliberate colour look per level in the grade pass we already run) | Good | Low to medium | It costs almost nothing. Most of the video's mood is the grade. Risk: the reserved colours (Foe Crimson, Ember warnings, Violet) must still read, so each land's grade needs a colour check. |
| **D. Lasting combat marks** (scorch and crack decals that stay on the floor until the room clears, like the bullet holes) | Fair | Medium | Cheap if pooled, and it adds a sense of history to a fight. Risk: floor clutter competing with floor telegraphs. Needs the VFX rules. |
| **E. Denser, lower-detail props** with one clear art direction per room | Good | Low | The video's hotel is mostly simple shapes, placed with care. That is our existing dressing system, not a new technique. |
| F. PBR materials and an environment map | Poor | High | It would drop the toon ramp and ink identity Kesh ruled on (BUILD_BRIEF §19.2). A glossy rim on metal parts is the most I'd take. |
| G. Floor reflections (planar or SSR) | Poor | High | 3 to 8 ms, and from above a mirror floor doubles the visual noise under telegraphs. |
| H. Depth of field, motion blur, constant chromatic aberration | Poor | Medium | These are FPS camera tricks. Top-down they blur the fight. A short CA or blur pulse on a big hit is the most that fits, and that is VFX work. |
| I. Screen-space AO | Poor | Medium | 2 to 5 ms on Iris Xe, and it muddies the ink edges. Option B gives most of the look for free. |

## Options once the freeze lifts

1. **Sun shadows (A)**: one fitted shadow map behind a graphics setting, shot on every land and timed on both laptops.
2. **Baked dressing AO (B)**: vertex AO in the procedural dressings, computed at room build.
3. **Per-land golden-hour grade (C)**: one grade per land in the existing grade pass, checked against the reserved colours.
4. **Lasting combat marks (D)**: pooled floor decals that clear with the room.
5. **Nothing.** Keep this as a reference only.

My pick would be 2 then 1. Option 2 is free at run time and low risk. Option 1 is the biggest visible gain, but it needs a laptop timing pass before it can ship.

## Shadow test (2026-11-11, throwaway build, nothing committed)

I switched on a 2048 sun shadow map in a local build at v0.5.360: the existing key light casts,
every opaque mesh receives, the sim is frozen, and the same frame is shot with and without
shadows. The shots were seen in software GL only, at about 1 fps.

- `shadow-test/level1-today-shadows-strongsun.jpg`: a Level I fight, shot three ways (today,
  shadows on, shadows plus 2.5x sun and half fill). The floor barely changes. Average frame
  brightness is 47.0, then 45.3, then 46.1. What does change is animated VFX.
- `shadow-test/camp-today-shadows-strongsun.jpg`: camp, the same three ways. With today's lights
  the shadows barely show. With a sun 3x stronger and fill at 0.4x, soft shadows appear behind
  tents, the cart and the rocks, but the whole camp turns bluer and harsher.
- A Level III shot was not taken: a fresh profile could not start Level III.

**Why the shadows don't show:** our rooms are not lit by the sun. In camp the sun is 1.15 against
a sky fill of 2.5, plus four torch lights. In the Level I room it is 1.76 against 1.61. The
bright areas you see are painted into the floor canvas and laid on top as glow pools, which
shadows can't darken. A shadow map would only darken a sun that supplies about a third of the
light.

**What that means:** sun shadows are not a switch. They would need every land relit to a
strong-sun, weak-fill rig, and every land's colour tuning measured again against the reserved
colours. That is a large job. A cheaper idea that matches how we already light things is a
**painted drop shadow**: a soft dark decal stretched away from the sun under each body and tall
prop, like the contact blobs we already have. It needs no shadow map and costs about nothing.
